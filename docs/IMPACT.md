# Societal impact brief (judging criterion: "Will it make people's lives better?")

**Purpose:** back the *Societal impact* criterion (docs/HACKATHON.md) with published, cited facts about US small
businesses and cash-flow shocks, and say plainly what FAULTLINE does and does not do about them.
**Written:** 2026-11-13 for issue #77. Every figure below was read by the author on the source page on the access date
shown, and is quoted as printed. Provenance of the facts: `REAL HISTORICAL` (published statistics). Nothing in this
brief is FAULTLINE output.
**Review:** ☑ Yaseen (finance owner) has checked each figure and its wording against the source (2026-11-14; the BLS figures S1, S2 were checked in a browser, since BLS blocks automated tools).

## How to read the numbers
- **All facts are US-only.**
- **"Small" is defined differently by each source.** BLS (facts 1–2) uses *firms* with fewer than 250 employees. The Fed
  survey (facts 6–8) covers *employer firms* with 1–499 employees. JPMorgan Chase Institute (facts 4–5) studies small
  businesses that bank with Chase. Fact 3 counts *establishments* (single locations), not firms.
- **Different samples, different years.** Facts 4 and 5 come from different samples, so they don't show a trend. The Fed
  survey is a convenience sample, and its own page says: "The SBCS is not a random sample; results should be analyzed
  with awareness of potential biases" that are "associated with convenience samples."

**What the facts do not say.** None of these sources says how many businesses fail *because of* a rate, demand or
supply shock. Fact 3 counts establishments that stopped operating for any reason. Facts 4–5 show how short the cushion
is if money stops coming in, not how often that happens.

## Why FAULTLINE helps (and what it does not do)

Small businesses account for slightly less than half of private-sector employment, and many hold less than a month of cash (facts 1, 4, 5),
so the *week* a shock hits cash matters as much as its size. FAULTLINE is a decision-support and stress-testing tool,
shown on a **SYNTHETIC** three-store food retailer. Today, on `main`, its CASCADE stress test runs a rate shock
(+200bp), a six-week 20% demand drop, a three-week supplier outage, or all three together through a deterministic cash
engine (no AI does the arithmetic). It shows the lowest cash point, the week it happens and the runway, labeled
`SIMULATED` and `SYNTHETIC`, so an operator can check that low point against the cash floor they would not go below
before living through the shock. Where data is missing or too thin, screens say `NOT AVAILABLE` or `INSUFFICIENT DATA`
instead of guessing. The recovery optimizer (#69, issue #17) and its live Protect payroll switch (#127) are on `main`:
the optimizer picks the least harmful mix of recovery actions that keeps cash at or above
the floor every week, or says `NO FEASIBLE PLAN`. With Protect payroll on, the payroll-cut variable's upper bound is set
to zero inside the solver (MATH_MODEL §4), so no plan can cut payroll. FAULTLINE does not prevent failures, give
financial advice, lend, trade, or change anyone's employment. It shows consequences; people decide.

## Three pitch-ready sentences (for the demo script, #46)

1. "Firms with fewer than 250 employees account for slightly less than half of America's private-sector jobs, according
   to the Bureau of Labor Statistics, and, in a separate JPMorgan Chase Institute study of its small-business customers, the median held enough
   cash to cover only 27 days of its typical outflows if money stopped coming in."
2. "FAULTLINE lets an operator run a rate shock, a demand drop or a supplier outage through the business before it
   happens and see the exact week cash hits its low point, computed by a deterministic engine on a synthetic business
   that is labeled as one."
3. "With Protect payroll on, the optimizer is not allowed to cut payroll at all, because that is a hard constraint in
   the solver, and if no plan keeps cash above the floor, it says NO FEASIBLE PLAN instead of a comforting answer."
